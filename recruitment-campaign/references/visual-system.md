# Visual System

## Start with a visual proposition

Write one sentence before selecting imagery:

> The visual world should make the candidate feel ___ by showing ___ in a way that is recognizably this employer.

The sentence must connect candidate emotion, employer mechanism, and brand identity. “Premium,” “futuristic,” “young,” and “artistic” are not sufficient propositions.

Add a second sentence that names the exclusion:

> It must not look like ___, because that would misstate the work or collapse the employer into an industry cliché.

This forces the direction to be specific enough to reject tempting but wrong imagery.

## Three different subjects

Do not confuse these:

1. **Brand philosophy** — what the employer believes is worth doing and how work should feel.
2. **Product or business expression** — how that belief becomes something customers or society can experience.
3. **Recruitment proposition** — why a person should enter and what they may contribute.

Brand philosophy may set the campaign's mood and central metaphor. Product or business expression can provide evidence. Composition and hierarchy must still address the applicant rather than sell the offering.

Remove the applicant layer as a test. If the hero image and message still work unchanged as a consumer, product, or corporate-brand advertisement, they cannot carry the recruitment campaign on their own.

## Give every image a job

Each major image should perform one function:

- **Summon** — create desire to enter.
- **Recognize** — mirror the candidate's tension.
- **Transition** — move between emotional chapters.
- **Prove** — show a real environment, tool, craft, or mechanism.
- **Orient** — clarify roles, standards, or the application path.
- **Echo** — leave meaning after the CTA.

Remove an image that has no job. Do not use people as a default symbol of “talent.” A person is useful when their action, environment, scale, or relationship proves something about the work.

## Build the system

Define and reuse:

- aspect ratio and page grid;
- safe margins;
- headline/body/caption scale;
- Chinese and Latin type roles;
- palette with functional contrast;
- image treatment and crop logic;
- numbering, rules, labels, and footer behavior;
- motion or interaction principles if applicable.

Consistency means a coherent system, not identical pages. Vary composition inside the same rules.

Lock the system before generating a full visual set. At minimum, record:

- one dominant visual motif tied to the work;
- one supporting motif for navigation or evidence;
- palette roles, not just color swatches;
- Chinese and Latin type roles;
- page or viewport rhythm;
- text-safe zones and crop behavior at target sizes;
- what may vary by role group and what must remain constant.

Do not solve a weak page by changing its palette or adding another decorative image. Repair the image's job, the hierarchy, or the underlying narrative beat.

## Image direction

Prefer imagery that is specific, tactile, and open to interpretation: material transformation, scale, trace, threshold, network, field, tool, unfinished construction, or a real work condition. Choose the motif only if it supports the proposition.

Avoid:

- generic smiling teams;
- decorative portraits with no work context;
- random stars, planets, doors, roads, or landscapes copied from another campaign;
- glossy product renders that erase the recruitment message;
- low-contrast mood overlays that destroy legibility;
- texture for texture's sake;
- one stock image stretched across every beat.

When generating images, specify subject, material, light, camera distance, negative space, brand palette, intended text zone, and exclusions. Do not ask merely for “cinematic premium recruitment poster.”

For a full-bleed background, also specify:

- the exact target aspect ratio;
- where the focal detail belongs;
- how much uninterrupted contrast the copy needs;
- the expected desktop and mobile crops;
- which objects, people, products, colors, and genre clichés must be absent.

Inspect the generated image at the final crop with real copy. A beautiful source image is not a successful background when the crop hides its meaning or makes the text unreadable.

When the hero depends on generated or newly commissioned imagery and the direction is genuinely uncertain, produce two meaningfully different candidates and select against the written visual proposition. Do not generate variants merely to decorate a settled direction.

## Work evidence before decorative identity

When the campaign risks becoming a product or lifestyle advertisement, show the work becoming real: traces, tools, materials, interfaces, decisions, tolerances, transitions, environments, or consequences. Use people only when their action or relationship is the proof.

The background must carry a proposition, not merely a texture. Ask what the viewer learns from it about the employer, the work, or the role. If the answer is only “this feels premium,” the image is not finished.

Do not present conceptual imagery as proof of a verified work mechanism. Map proof visuals to a sourced work object, action, environment, relation, or consequence. Label speculative diagrams, mock documents, and generated process scenes as conceptual when a viewer could mistake them for evidence.

## Three visual gates

Pass all three before finalizing an image-led campaign:

1. **Logo-off test** — hide the logo and headline. Can you still explain why the visual belongs to this employer from the work subject or system, not only the category?
2. **Consumer-ad test** — remove recruitment labels. If the visual becomes a finished product or corporate advertisement with no work entry point, it cannot carry the campaign alone.
3. **Actual-crop test** — inspect desktop and mobile crops with final text. The subject, negative space, contrast, and reading order must survive.

Failure is a reason to change the visual thesis, asset, crop, or hierarchy—not simply to add more overlay.

## Hierarchy checks

At a glance, a cover should reveal:

1. the employer;
2. that this is recruitment;
3. the opportunity or audience;
4. one line worth entering for.

On closing pages, the action should be easy to find before the philosophical echo. The echo may be quiet; it must not compete with the CTA.

For a campaign website, the first viewport should contain employer, recruitment signal, scope or audience, mother proposition, and a route forward. The main line should feel deliberate but should not fill the entire screen by default. Test scale against the actual background, not an empty artboard.

## Reference handling

Analyze references for principles such as restraint, scale contrast, rhythm, negative space, image-text relationship, and narrative pacing. Do not reproduce distinctive artwork, signature composition, logo treatment, or recognizable phrasing.

The target is equivalent conviction with employer-specific meaning, not visual cosplay.

## Continuity audit

View the complete sequence at thumbnail scale, then at reading scale.

At thumbnail scale, check that page heights, margins, palette behavior, image treatment, and type hierarchy feel like one system. At reading scale, check that emphasis follows the narrative rather than repeating the same poster in new colors.

A coherent campaign may alternate quiet and intense pages. It should not alternate between unrelated art directions, arbitrary aspect ratios, or consumer advertising and recruitment graphics.
