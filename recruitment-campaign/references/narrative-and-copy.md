# Narrative and Copy

## Narrative spine

For a full campaign, build an ordered experience:

1. **Invitation** — signal that this is recruitment and name the opportunity.
2. **Recognition** — articulate something the desired candidate already feels.
3. **Reframe** — offer a credible different way to see work or growth.
4. **Proof** — reveal the operating mechanism behind that promise.
5. **Responsibility** — show what the candidate can actually own.
6. **Standard** — show how good work is judged.
7. **Gain** — state what the person can become, learn, earn, or experience without inventing outcomes.
8. **Fit** — let the right people recognize themselves and the wrong people self-select out.
9. **Action** — make the next step concrete.
10. **Aftertaste** — close with a resonant image or sentence after the action is already clear.

Short formats may compress beats, but should retain recognition, promise, proof, and action.

Do not convert the arc into ten slogan cards. Each beat should change the applicant's state: from curiosity, to recognition, to belief, to self-location, to action. If two adjacent sections make the same emotional claim, combine them or make one carry evidence.

## Let philosophy call; let work answer

Brand philosophy may be the campaign's emotional center or opening pull, but it is not a complete recruitment proposition by itself. Its job is to help the right applicants recognize a shared belief about meaningful work, growth, responsibility, or change. Do not strip it away in the name of practicality.

Translate the belief into an applicant-facing choice: why this belief needs people, what they may own, how real work proves it, and what action lets them enter. Then it becomes a lived opportunity rather than a customer-facing slogan.

Use this conversion:

| Company statement | Recruitment conversion |
|---|---|
| We believe in X | If you share X, this is the part of it you can help make real |
| We built X | You may contribute to this concrete part |
| We value X | Your work will be enabled and judged through this mechanism |
| The industry is changing | Here is the change you can help make real |

If the applicant disappears from the sentence, continue translating. If the philosophy disappears and only duties remain, restore the reason the work is worth choosing.

## Applicant grammar test

Read the campaign once with this question: who is the grammatical subject?

- If the employer is always “we” and the applicant only appears at the CTA, it is corporate brand communication.
- If the product is the hero and work appears as backstage trivia, it is product communication.
- If “you” is praised without being given a real decision, standard, or responsibility, it is flattery.
- Recruitment language connects the applicant's point of view to a credible part they can play.

Use role groups when one mother proposition must recruit different kinds of talent. Keep the proposition shared, but give each group its own `your part`, `what must hold`, work proof, and action route. Do not flatten creative, technical, operational, commercial, and frontline work into one generic promise.

## Campaign language

- Prefer verbs, concrete nouns, and observable consequences.
- Alternate short declarative lines with enough explanation to remain clear.
- Give a line visual emphasis because it advances the argument, not merely because it sounds quotable.
- Use contrast only when both sides are true and meaningful.
- Treat “you” as a real candidate with choices, not an abstract hero.
- State selectivity through standards, responsibility, and proof rather than elitist posturing.

## English names and lines

An English name is optional. Use one only when it helps the audience, brand, or channel. When used, prefer simple, legible English that survives pronunciation and translation. Two or three familiar words often outperform a coined term. Test:

1. Can a non-native reader understand it without a paragraph?
2. Does it describe agency, invitation, or a useful tension?
3. Could ten unrelated employers use it unchanged?
4. Does the Chinese rendering carry the same force rather than merely translate words?

Run a cold-reader paraphrase on slogans, role labels, and unfamiliar terms: could someone outside the project explain the phrase in one ordinary Chinese sentence? If not, simplify it or explain it at first use. Do not preserve opaque English merely because it looks sophisticated.

## Literary and philosophical material

Quotations are optional. Use one only when it deepens the campaign's central tension.

Verify:

- exact wording;
- author;
- work;
- relevant context;
- a reliable source.

Prefer a short excerpt. Keep the quotation visually subordinate to the employer's own proposition. Do not misattribute a popular paraphrase. If exact verification is not possible, use an original line and do not present it as a quotation.

Good placements are the opening threshold or final echo. Avoid using quotations as decoration on every page.

An original philosophical line can be safer and more ownable than a famous quotation. Label it as campaign original when readers might otherwise infer attribution. Keep the line quieter than the employer proposition and, at the close, quieter than the action.

## Common copy failures

- a chain of posters with no narrative movement;
- vague universals such as “define the future” or “break all boundaries”;
- explaining openness as absence of standards;
- turning one attractive sentence into repeated color variations;
- a philosophical manifesto followed by a disconnected apply button;
- candidate vulnerability used as emotional bait;
- imitation of a reference brand's signature phrase or rhythm.

Edit until each section either increases desire, increases belief, or reduces action friction.

## Final copy pressure test

For every prominent sentence, ask:

1. Could a close competitor publish it unchanged?
2. Does it advance the argument or only sound expensive?
3. Is the abstraction paid for by a nearby fact, mechanism, or responsibility?
4. Would a non-native English reader understand the campaign line immediately?
5. Does the Chinese carry the same intention and force rather than mirror the word order?

Delete or rewrite a line that fails two or more questions.
