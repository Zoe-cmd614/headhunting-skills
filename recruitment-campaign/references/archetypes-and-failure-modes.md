# Archetypes and Failure Modes

Use these as diagnostic starting points, not templates. Cases are evidence about structure, never permission to copy their names, phrases, colors, or imagery.

## Archetype A: high-autonomy small platform

**Candidate tension:** traditional entry roles offer observation and fragments rather than consequential ownership.

**Recruitment promise:** real responsibility with freedom over the path.

**Proof needed:** access, decision scope, feedback loops, operating flexibility, and a visible result standard.

**Typical failure:** celebrating freedom without explaining accountability, or turning KPI into a threatening visual spectacle.

## Archetype B: complex-technology employer

**Candidate tension:** the mission and products are impressive, but an individual cannot see where their contribution enters.

**Recruitment promise:** a specific part of a difficult system can become yours to advance.

**Proof needed:** technical problems, interfaces between disciplines, craft standards, development cycles, and contribution paths.

**Typical failure:** a beautiful product or corporate-brand film with recruitment language appended at the end.

## Archetype C: broad frontline employer

**Candidate tension:** role lists and org charts flatten varied work into generic vacancies.

**Recruitment promise:** enter through a real arena, customer problem, craft, or field of play.

**Proof needed:** daily situations, autonomy boundaries, skills built, progression, team rhythms, and immediate outcomes.

**Typical failure:** lifestyle imagery without the truth of the work, or a slogan that makes every role sound identical.

## Archetype D: multi-talent industrial system

**Candidate tension:** one large employer needs very different people, yet a single campaign often erases their distinct standards.

**Recruitment promise:** one shared consequential system, with a different meaningful contribution path for each talent sequence.

**Proof needed:** a mother proposition plus separate narratives, work mechanisms, and standards for research, engineering, operations, manufacturing, commercial, or other verified groups.

**Typical failure:** one heroic line and one visual language applied unchanged to every audience.

## Diagnostic anti-patterns

### Product communication mistaken for philosophy-led recruitment

The page uses products, specifications, or customer benefits as if they were a reason to join. Recover the deeper belief, address it to applicants, and show what they can help make real. Keep brand philosophy; remove the sales logic.

### A person used as shorthand for philosophy

A portrait or silhouette creates “human” mood but supplies no evidence about work. Replace it with an action, environment, material, or relation that carries meaning.

### Generic premium

Elegant serif type, muted colors, cinematic imagery, and large whitespace create taste without identity. Re-anchor every choice in the employer mechanism and candidate motive or tension.

### Quote-led concept

A literary sentence becomes more memorable than the employer. Reduce it to threshold or aftertaste and restore the employer's own proposition.

### Mood mistaken for a visual thesis

The images are elegant, cinematic, or expensive-looking but could advertise any employer in the category. Replace category atmosphere with an employer-specific subject that shows a work mechanism, decision, material, environment, or consequence.

### Background without a job

A full-bleed image fills space but does not summon, prove, orient, transition, or echo. Give it a clear narrative function, an intentional text-safe zone, and a crop that preserves meaning. Otherwise remove it.

### Incoherent page sequence

Each page looks polished alone, but scales, aspect ratios, palettes, and image genres drift. Lock system invariants, inspect the sequence at thumbnail scale, and vary composition inside one visual grammar.

### Concept mistaken for official communication

An independent exercise uses a real employer's name or identity without clarifying status. Add an unobtrusive disclosure and avoid claims or brand assets that imply authorization.

### High-halo employer hides the work

A famous worldview, founder, mission, product, or cultural status creates attraction, but candidates outside the most visible roles cannot locate their contribution. Keep the halo as an entrance only when it has recruiting value, then reveal distinct work mechanisms, standards, and contribution paths. Do not prescribe one fixed promise or visual metaphor across cultural, craft, technology, and public-interest employers.

### All roles flattened

The campaign gains consistency but loses relevance. Preserve the mother proposition while tailoring proof and standards by talent group.

### Recruitment appended to a brand campaign

An apply button appears after a company story that never addressed a candidate. Rebuild the story with the candidate as grammatical subject.

### CTA mismatch

The campaign promises a broad opportunity but routes to a narrow or inactive destination. Align copy, eligibility, destination, and post-click expectation.

## Selection guide

Choose the closest archetype based on the employer's hiring problem, not its industry. Hybrid campaigns may combine archetypes, but keep one primary tension and one mother proposition. If none fits, derive a new archetype from verified evidence instead of forcing the employer into these four.
